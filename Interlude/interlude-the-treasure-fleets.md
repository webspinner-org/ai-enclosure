# Interlude: The Treasure Fleets

*Suggested placement: between Part IV (The Versus) and Part V (The Mission). Functions as the book's pivot from diagnosis to invitation.*

---

In the summer of 1433, in the city of Nanjing, a small group of Ming court officials began the work of erasing the records of the world's greatest navy.

For nearly thirty years, Admiral Zheng He had commanded fleets that defied the imagination of every other civilization on Earth. Three hundred ships. Sixty of them larger than any European vessel that would be built for the next four centuries. Crews of nearly thirty thousand men. Voyages that reached India, Arabia, the east coast of Africa. The flagship of the fleet, by surviving accounts, was over four hundred feet long — a length European shipbuilding would not approach until the nineteenth century. Chinese cartographers were drawing shorelines no European had yet seen. Chinese navigators understood the monsoons, the longitude problem, and the celestial mechanics of long-distance voyaging at a level no other civilization would attain for two hundred years.

China, in 1433, was the most technologically advanced civilization on the planet. Its shipbuilders worked in dry docks Europe could not match. Its smiths produced cast iron of a quality Europe would reach only in the eighteenth century. Its printers had been using movable type for four centuries; its merchants had been using paper currency for almost as long. Gunpowder, the magnetic compass, the stern-post rudder, the watertight bulkhead — all Chinese inventions, all centuries ahead of European parallels. The treasure fleets were not an outlier. They were the maritime expression of a civilization that had, by every meaningful measure, won the technology race.

And then, the court withdrew.

There were reasons. There always are. The fleets were ruinously expensive. The eunuch faction that ran them had political enemies. The Confucian scholar-officials who controlled court ideology held that virtuous trade was conducted by foreigners coming to the Middle Kingdom, not by the Middle Kingdom going abroad. The Mongol threat to the north demanded resources. The Grand Canal was complete, internal trade was robust, the empire was vast. *Why*, the argument went, *should the empire seek what already comes to it?*

So the records were burned. The shipyards were closed. Construction of ocean-going vessels was banned. Within a generation, the knowledge was lost — not destroyed, but unmaintained, unpracticed, allowed to atrophy in libraries no one was permitted to read. Within two generations, China's coastal capability had decayed to the point that Japanese pirates raided its shores with impunity.

The cost did not arrive immediately. Empires are large; the consequences of decisions made in Beijing rolled out over centuries. For the next four hundred years, China remained wealthy, populous, and culturally formidable. Its withdrawal from the sea was not visible to the Chinese themselves. The world simply went on without it.

Then, in 1839, a British fleet arrived in the South China Sea, carrying technologies — steam-powered iron ships, percussion-cap rifles, explosive shells — that descended from a tradition of maritime engineering Britain had inherited from Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands, all of whom had inherited it from the slow, decentralized, irrepressibly competitive ferment of European seafaring. China, which had been four centuries ahead in 1433, was now four centuries behind. The Opium Wars followed. The Unequal Treaties followed. The Boxer Rebellion. The carving of China into European concessions. The Century of Humiliation — a phrase still taught in Chinese schools — had begun.

It is difficult to overstate how deeply this story is etched into the historical consciousness of contemporary China. Every Chinese schoolchild learns it. Every Chinese strategist references it. The decision the Ming court made in 1433 is the foundational cautionary tale of modern Chinese statecraft — the *never again* that organizes everything from the Belt and Road Initiative to the country's investment in semiconductor manufacturing to the Made in China 2025 strategy.

Which makes what is happening now all the more remarkable.

---

China is, by most measures, one of the world's two leading developers of synthetic intelligence. Its labs are well-funded, its researchers are talented, its data sets are vast, its hardware ambitions are aggressive, and its national strategy explicitly positions SI as a generational technology no nation can afford to miss. China learned the Ming lesson. China is determined never to repeat it.

And yet.

The lesson China learned was not *democratize the synthetic mind*. The lesson China learned was *do not let another civilization get ahead of us*. The response, accordingly, has been a state-coordinated, state-directed, state-controlled push for synthetic intelligence as an instrument of national power. State-aligned SI champions. State-aligned research priorities. State-aligned export controls. State-aligned applications: surveillance, military targeting, social-credit scoring, propaganda, content moderation at billion-user scale.

This is not a critique unique to China. The United States is, in its own register, doing the same thing. The major American SI laboratories are increasingly indistinguishable from defense contractors, with multibillion-dollar contracts to integrate their systems into military planning, intelligence analysis, and weapons targeting. The European Union is racing to fund its own "sovereign AI" champions on national-security grounds. India, the United Kingdom, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates — every nation with the capital to play is now building or buying a state-aligned SI capability, and the language they use to justify it is identical: *we cannot afford to fall behind.*

This is the deepest irony of all. The Ming lesson — *do not withdraw from a transformative technology* — has been remembered. The opposite lesson, the one the personal computer taught the world fifty years ago — *democratize, distribute, decentralize, or watch the technology be captured by those who already hold the capital* — has been forgotten.

What we are watching, in real time, is the construction of a synthetic-intelligence arms race conducted between a small number of states and the corporate champions they have anointed. Each champion controls compute, data, models, and distribution, and is increasingly involved in shaping the regulatory framework that governs all of it. Each state insists that this concentration is necessary for national security. Each champion insists that this concentration is necessary for "AI safety." Each pretends that the alternative — a synthetic mind owned, operated, and inspected by ordinary people — is either impossible or dangerous.

It is neither.

---

The personal computer did not democratize itself. It was democratized, against the wishes of every actor who profited from the prior arrangement.

In 1975, the conventional wisdom held that real computing required institutional ownership. Mainframes lived in glass-walled rooms. Operators wore white coats. The IBM monopoly was so total it had become invisible — the way water is invisible to the fish that swim in it. The idea that a person of ordinary means might own a computer, run their own programs, store their own data, and answer to no central authority for any of it was, to most observers in 1975, a hobbyist fantasy.

The hobbyists won. They won not because they had more capital than IBM — they had almost none. They won not because they had governments on their side — they did not. They won because the technology had reached a point at which centralization was no longer necessary, and a generation of builders refused to accept the arrangement that had become customary. They built kit computers. They wrote software they gave away. They formed user groups, magazines, mail-order catalogs. They created an ecosystem the priesthood could not police because there was no center to it. Within ten years, the priesthood's monopoly was broken.

The same window is open now, with synthetic intelligence. And it is closing.

The technology has reached a point at which centralization is no longer necessary. Local models of impressive capability run on consumer hardware. Open weights are widely available. Embedding generation, vector retrieval, and the orchestration logic that makes synthetic intelligence useful do not require billion-dollar data centers. They require ordinary computers, ordinary networks, and the ordinary human willingness to coordinate.

What they require, more than anything, is people who refuse to accept the arrangement that is becoming customary. People who refuse to treat synthetic intelligence as something that legitimately belongs to a handful of state-aligned corporations. People who insist on a different default: that the synthetic mind, like the personal mind it serves, belongs to the person who uses it.

This is where you come in.

The Webspinner Foundation is not a company. It is a movement. Like the Homebrew Computer Club, like the Free Software movement, like the early World Wide Web, it is a coordinated refusal — a refusal to accept that synthetic intelligence must be enclosed, surveilled, conscripted into wars and surveillance regimes, or rented at exorbitant cost from the entities that have most aggressively claimed it. The Foundation exists to build, to share, to teach, and to operate the alternative.

Warp is the architecture that makes the alternative real. Cells are the units that ordinary people can build and operate, individually or in cooperation. WRAG is the technique that grounds synthetic intelligence in *your* knowledge rather than in the aggregated harvest of humanity's writing. Sovereign Intelligence is the goal: a synthetic mind that is yours, that cannot be repurposed without your consent, and that cannot be conscripted into wars or surveillance you do not endorse.

You do not need to be a programmer to join. You need only to refuse the customary arrangement. You can:

Five hundred years ago, a court in Nanjing burned the records of the world's greatest fleet because the powerful preferred a smaller, more controllable world. The cost of that decision was paid for four centuries by people who had no part in making it.

A similar decision is being made now, by a similar class of people, in service of a similar preference. The cost will be paid for centuries by people who have no part in the making of it — unless those people refuse.

The Webspinner Foundation invites you to refuse, with us, by building.
