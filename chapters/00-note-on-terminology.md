# A Note on Terminology

*Placement: immediately before the Executive Summary. This is the framing device for the entire book — the reader's first encounter with the language the rest of the book will speak.*

---

Throughout this book, you will encounter two terms that may be unfamiliar: **Synthetic Intelligence (SI)** and **Sovereign Intelligence**. Both choices are deliberate, and both matter to the argument.

We do not use *Artificial Intelligence*. The word *artificial* means fake, imitation, a copy of something real. An artificial flower is a lesser thing than a real flower. An artificial sweetener is a substitute for sugar. The word carries a quiet diminishment — a way of saying "this isn't quite serious, isn't quite real, it's happening over there in someone else's lab." That framing has cost us dearly.

The systems we now contend with are not artificial. They produce real outputs, with real effects, on real lives. They make consequential decisions about hiring, lending, sentencing, healthcare, and warfare. They generate the content read by hundreds of millions of people. They consume real water and real electricity at industrial scale. They concentrate real wealth and real power. There is nothing artificial about any of this except the word.

The Webspinner Foundation uses **Synthetic Intelligence** because *synthetic* tells the truth. Synthetic means *made by synthesis* — constructed by combining things, real but built rather than grown. A synthetic diamond is chemically identical to a mined one; it differs only in origin. The word honors what these systems are without pretending they are something they are not. It also clarifies the moral fact that *artificial* obscures: someone *made* this. From human output, by human design, for human purposes. Things that are made have makers. Makers have intentions. Intentions can be examined, contested, and held accountable. The fiction of "artificial" intelligence — the suggestion that it emerged on its own from the ether of computation — hides the question that this entire book asks: *who built what, for whom, and at whose expense?*

That brings us to the second term.

**Sovereign Intelligence** is what this book is for. The phrase carries two meanings, and both matter.

The first is synthetic: an intelligence *you* own, that *you* control, that *you* can inspect and modify and refuse — rather than one rented to you on terms you cannot read by entities you cannot reach. Sovereign Synthetic Intelligence is the technical thesis of this book, and Warp is the architecture that makes it real.

The second meaning is older and human: the sovereignty of your own mind. Your capacity to think your own thoughts, reach your own conclusions, decide what you believe and why. That capacity has been under pressure for longer than synthetic intelligence has existed — from advertising, from algorithms, from the attention economy — but the arrival of hyperscale SI represents the largest single threat to it in human history. When the systems that mediate your information, your work, your communication, and increasingly your reasoning are owned by a handful of entities accountable only to capital, your mind is no longer entirely your own.

Both sovereignties are at stake. Both require defense. The moral imperative this book argues for is simple: **the synthetic kind must be built in service of the human kind — not as its replacement, not as its competitor, and never as its master.**

Warp is the architecture that makes that service structurally possible. The AI Enclosure is what happens if we don't build it in time.

When you see *SI* in these pages, read it as Synthetic Intelligence — the constructed kind, with all its real consequences. When you see *Sovereign Intelligence*, read it as the goal: both kinds, defended together.

That is the work this book proposes. Warp speed is the pace it requires.
