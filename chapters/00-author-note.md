# A Note on Authorship

There are three things you should know before you read another word of this book.

The first is who wrote it.

I did. I am John D. Marx, founder of the Webspinner Foundation, and the argument in these pages is mine. I have lived it for more than forty years, am living it now, and intend to spend whatever working years remain to me defending it. The thesis, the structure, the moral case, the technical claims, the title, the antagonist, the four pillars, the Value Triangle, the language of Cells and Looms and Weavers and Grimoires — all of it is mine. I will answer for every paragraph.

But I did not type every paragraph.

That is the second thing you should know. This book has been written, line by line, with the help of a rented AI — a commercial service that I pay for, that runs on someone else's hardware, in someone else's data center, on terms I did not write. I have spent the last two years building software with these tools, and I will tell you plainly: I do not believe I could have produced this book in the time I had to give it without their help. So I asked for help. The book exists because I asked for help.

The irony is loud, and I want to name it before anyone else does. *The book that argues for putting synthetic intelligence in the hands of ordinary people, owned by them, on their own terms, is itself being written with a rented intelligence I do not own and cannot fully inspect.* I see no reason to hide that. It is, in its own way, the strongest argument for the work. If the case against rented intelligence has to be drafted with rented intelligence, the case is not academic. It is the condition of the moment.

The third thing you should know is how I am keeping that condition honest.

There is a small typographic tell that contemporary AI models leave in their prose. They love the em-dash — like this. Edited texts often strip them out, both to disguise the AI's hand and because their overuse is a real stylistic flaw. I have chosen, deliberately, not to strip them out of this book. Where you see an em-dash, read it as a small flag I am leaving for you and for myself. It is my reminder, on every page, that I am the moral compass for this work and the AI is not. I will not let the AI guess. I will not let the AI drift. I will not let the AI lie. What I am controlling is not the AI itself — that fight is above my pay grade — but the work product the two of us produce together. Every sentence has passed through me before it passed to you. The em-dashes are my way of saying so.

---

A word about why I am using these tools at all.

I have freely chosen to embrace AI. I want to say that plainly. And I want to say, just as plainly, that the choice was less free than I might have liked.

I have spent my career in information technology. Over the last two decades, much of the work I once did, and much of the work my peers once did, has migrated offshore in the service of cost reduction and global supply-chain efficiency. The roles within IT have become commodity labor priced at the lowest global denominator. There are people of my generation who responded to that compression by withdrawing. My nature is to engage. So I pivoted.

Two years into building seriously with these tools, I have seen something I did not expect. The commoditization is not stopping at IT roles. It is going to come for software itself. The applications and platforms that today justify the trillion-dollar valuations of a small handful of corporations are about to become, in many of their functions, generatable on demand. The disruption is not going to arrive only from unicorn startups, in the familiar pattern of the last twenty years. It is going to arrive from the technology itself, and it will arrive faster than the large corporations are accustomed to adapting to. They are, by structure and by habit, slow.

For most of the last forty years, the way large companies have absorbed disruption is by buying the disruptor. The Wall Street machinery of mergers and acquisitions has been the off-ramp from creative destruction. That machinery does not work here. AI is not a company you can buy. It is not a product you can acquire and shelve and reskin. It is a capability that has to be *made*, and made in the place where it will be used, on terms the user can defend.

That is the premise of what the Foundation calls Sovereign Intelligence. We must own this. Not "we" the nation-state. Not "we" the corporation. Not "we" the platform. *We* — the user, the family, the small business, the cooperative, the community, the person. If we do not own it, the few entities that do own it will own us through it. There is no third option.

---

A last word, and then I will get out of the way.

I am proud to use these tools. I am proud of what they let me do. I do not wish to pretend otherwise, and I do not wish to mislead anyone — least of all the reader who has trusted me with this much of their attention. The fact that I am writing a manifesto for sovereign intelligence with the help of an unsovereign one is, to me, another piece of evidence that there is a Sovereign Power over all of us with a particular and persistent sense of humor. Mine is God. Not AI.

The Foreword that follows is mine, in my voice, fact-checked by me, signed by me. Every word in this book is in that condition. The help I have had has been substantial. The responsibility I am claiming is total.

— John D. Marx
   Founder, The Webspinner Foundation
